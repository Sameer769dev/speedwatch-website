Wi-Fi vs. Ethernet: How Much Speed & Latency Do You Actually Lose?

Published by SpeedWatch Labs · Updated September 16, 2026 · 8 min read

"Is a wired cable really that much faster than modern Wi-Fi 6 or Wi-Fi 7?"

With router marketing promising theoretical speeds up to "19 Gbps," many consumers assume that connecting an Ethernet cable is an obsolete practice. But when you look at real-world benchmarks, the physics of radio waves tell a radically different story.

Here is how Wi-Fi compares to Ethernet across real-world speed tests, latency, jitter, and packet loss.

1. Half-Duplex vs. Full-Duplex Architecture

The fundamental difference between wireless and wired networking lies in how data moves through the physical medium:

  • Wi-Fi is Half-Duplex: Wireless devices share the same frequency spectrum. A device cannot transmit and receive simultaneously on the same channel. Only one device (your phone, your TV, your neighbor's router) can talk at any single microsecond. If two transmit at once, a packet collision occurs and data must be re-sent.
  • Ethernet is Full-Duplex: An 8-pin Cat6 cable has separate dedicated twisted copper pairs for sending and receiving data simultaneously. A 1,000 Mbps cable delivers 1,000 Mbps upload and 1,000 Mbps download simultaneously with zero collision delay.

3. Physical Obstacles: Wall and Material Attenuation

Higher Wi-Fi frequencies (5 GHz and 6 GHz) deliver higher raw speed, but struggle with obstacle penetration:

  • Drywall / Plasterboard: Causes a -3 dB to -5 dB loss in signal strength (cutting effective speed by ~25%).
  • Brick & Concrete Walls: Attenuates signal by -10 dB to -18 dB, dropping throughput by up to 75% or triggering fallback to slow 2.4 GHz.
  • Metal & Mirrors: Acts as a radio frequency mirror, causing multipath interference and ghosting packet collisions.
  • Cat6 Ethernet: Shielded against electromagnetic interference (EMI) with zero signal loss up to 100 meters (328 feet).

4. The Verdict: When Should You Use Which?

Always Use Ethernet For: Desktop PCs, gaming consoles (PlayStation, Xbox), 4K home theater streaming boxes (Apple TV, Nvidia Shield), and Network Attached Storage (NAS).

Use 5GHz/6GHz Wi-Fi For: Smartphones, tablets, e-readers, smart home automation, and laptops when mobility around the house is required.
